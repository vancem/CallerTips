# No Experience Necessary Dances

It is likely at some point you will need to call a ‘No Experience Necessary
Dance’. At this dance you assume that the dancers know NOTHING, and you teach
them everything they need on the spot. These dances are typically 2 hours.
Calling one is really quite different from a normal dance. It is almost EXACTLY
what you do at a first square dance lesson. The good news is that the
choreography is VERY simple. You typically teach only circle moves (everyone
stays in a circle or a squared set, and you never form lines). The dancers never
get scrambled so so you do tha have to have mastered resolution yet. A few
things about these dances are significantly harder, though:

1. You need to have a VERY CLEAR idea of what you are going to teach and in what
   order. You want to ‘keep things moving’ which means you always have to know
   what you are going to teach next.
2. You have to WATCH your squares carefully and ADAPT your calling to the
   dancers (slowing down as needed). Do NOT get hung up on finishing figures ‘on
   time with the music’.
3. You want to PRAISE the dancers when they succeed, and NEVER LET FRUSTRATION
   come into your voice on the mic. PATIENCE, REASSURANCE and ENTHUSIASM are the
   only emotions you should show the dancers.

The order of things

1. **Get them into squares**. This works better with angels (experienced dancers
   who are there to help the newcomers). Ask each angel to find a newcomer and
   bring them into a square. The angels will take it from there. If you have
   even one square formed, you can point to it and ask the rest to replicate it.
   If you have no angels, tell people to couple up with the boy on the girl’s
   left, and to find three other couples to face, so the four couples form a
   circle or a square. Then tell them to line up with the walls of the building.
   The hardest part is when many people do not have a partner yet. They mill
   around, hesitant to couple up. Ask them to raise their hand if they need a
   partner. If you have a gender imbalance, you can point out that anyone can
   fill either role, but warn them that the roles ARE different and that each
   dancer should stick with one role the whole dance. If a girl would have to
   dance the boy’s part, have an angel do it.

2. **Teach partner and corner, and squared set and home**. Next, tell them about
   their partner (the person they coupled up with, standing beside them) and
   their corner (the person beside them on the other side). Ask them to
   introduce themselves to both, and to remember who those people are. Tell them
   that this formation is a squared set, and that home is the spot they are
   standing in, facing the way they are facing now. Then teach heads and sides.
   The heads are the couple facing you and the couple opposite them, which is
   the usual setup when you stand at a head wall. The other two couples are the
   sides. Have the heads step into the middle and back out, then have the sides
   do the same.

3. Teach them hand-holds. **Boys** reach out to their partner, **palm UP**.
   Girls reach out to their partner, **palm DOWN**. All hand holds are OPEN. You
   are not grasping, just touching cupped palms.

**Tip:** while Dosado is traditionally one of the very first calls taught, **I
DO NOT use Dosado in my no-experience-necessary dances**. The reason is that it
is an AWKWARD move (you pass back to back, then back up), and angels will be
tempted to turn it into a variation, such as a swing or a highland fling. It
also does NOT set you up for an Allemande Left. At the end of a dosado you are
facing your partner, not your corner. For all these reasons I **use TURN YOUR
PARTNER BY THE RIGHT** instead of dosado in the early choreography.

**Tip:** **Minimize the use of LEFT and RIGHT in any explanations**. MOST
people, including myself, can’t effortlessly point left or right on command
(they have to think about it). INSTEAD, when a RIGHT hand is needed, say
**SHAKE**. Most people will EFFORTLESSLY reach with the right hand when asked to
shake hands. Avoid talking about LEFT. The only time you will be tempted is when
explaining Allemande Left. INSTEAD, tell them to face AWAY from their partner.
BOYS reach out with the hand closest to their corner. GIRLS reach ACROSS their
body with the hand they were holding their partner with. (Notice I never
mentioned left.) We don’t teach star thru at a no-experience-necessary dance.
When I teach it elsewhere, I say EVERYONE reaches out with the hand they were
holding their partner with. Again, there is no need to talk about left or right.

**Tip:** **Teach pass thru (or any no-hands move) by teaching pull by (the
handed version of the call) first.** When I learned pass-thru they said to ‘pass
right shoulders’ which is correct, but has never been helpful to me in the
moment (because LEFT and RIGHT are not instinctive). So teach pass thru as a
pull by without hands. In the pull by, they reach out with the hand they shake
hands with. Notice this explains pass-thru without ever using the word ‘right’
or ‘left’.

**Tip:** **have the music on whenever you are not talking.** This is a dance,
and it is SIGNIFICANTLY more fun with the music on than with it off, so have it
on unless you are explaining something. **If the dancers are moving, the music
should be on**.

4. **Teach them ‘turn your partner by the right, allemande left’.** Face your
   partner, SHAKE hands, and walk around them holding hands. Then reach out with
   your other hand to your corner and turn that person. That turn is an
   allemande left. Repeat it, then call ‘square your sets.’

**Tip:** **Teach them ‘default’ actions.** When in doubt, WALK FORWARD and
ALTERNATE hands.

5. **Teach them promenade**. After an allemande left, reach out to your partner
   and SHAKE their hand (right hand). WITHOUT letting go, the girls turn to face
   the same direction as the boys. Then everyone joins left hands underneath the
   joined right hands. Walk around the square until you get home (where you
   started).

6. **Teach them right and left grand.** Face your partner, SHAKE hands, and pull
   by. Take the other hand with the next person and pull by. Keep alternating
   hands all the way around until you meet your partner again.

7. **Teach them circle left.** Have them join hands in one circle and walk in
   the same direction they just promenaded. Show one square, so you never have
   to say left or right.

   You can also start a figure with just ‘allemande left.’ Up to this point you
   have always told them to face their partner and turn by the right (shaking
   hands) first. An allemande left by itself is ALWAYS with the corner (away
   from their partner). Hopefully they get the correct hand from the muscle
   memory you have already built up.

8. **Teach them boys star right**. I find it easier to teach the heads first and
   the sides second. Ask the head boys to step FORWARD and touch INSIDE hands.
   Then ask the side boys to join them. Emphasize that they just walk forward
   and form a star with the inside hand. (Don’t teach the boys a left-hand star.
   You don’t need it, and it is harder).  Turn the star until they meet their
   corner, then allemande left, right and left grand, promenade home.

9. **Teach them girls star left**. Use the same procedure. Have just the head
   girls step forward and touch INSIDE hands, then have the side girls join
   them. Turn the star until they meet their partner, turn partner by the right,
   allemande left, right and left grand, promenade home.

10. **Do a SHORT singing call (explaining what it is).** Maybe only two or three
    ring figures (figures that stay in the circle), for example:

    - CIRC-L, AL, TURN-PART-R, AL, RLG, PROM
    - B-STAR-R, AL, RLG, PROM
    - G-STAR-L, TURN-PART-R, AL, RLG, PROM

    These abbreviations match the Taminations list: AL is allemande left, RLG is
    right and left grand.  

    BE CAREFUL NOT TO CALL SWING. End the record early: when the dancers finish,
    cut the music.

11. **Take a break between tips.** Tell them this is a rest, and that the dance
    will start again with the next tip.

12. **Review everything** on the next tip.

13. **Teach them heads (sides) right and left thru**. Teach them pull by (SHAKE
    HANDS, pull by). Then explain that you want them to turn around AS A COUPLE:
    the boys back up and the girls walk forward in a SMALL half-circle. Don’t
    worry about hands yet. Do that a couple of times SLOWLY. When the overall
    movement is right, add the hands. After the pull by, both dancers reach out
    with their left hand, as in a right and left grand, and touch hands (boy’s
    palm up, girl’s palm down). The girl places her other hand behind her back.
    The boy places his free (right) hand on the hand behind her back. The boys
    back up and the girls move forward, as before. Tell them that a right and
    left thru is a **pull by and a courtesy turn** (each of which is a real
    call).

14. **Teach them heads (sides) promenade ½.** Explain that the sides have to
    step back so the heads can walk past. Do H-PROM ½; H RLT; S-PROM ½; S RLT;
    Face corner, AL, RLG, PROM. This is a singing call figure. It runs short, so
    beginners have extra time to finish.

15. **Teach them ‘all into the middle with a cowboy yell.’** Everyone walks into
    the center, yells, and backs out. This is useful if you have extra time, and
    it is fun.

You now have enough to do a full singing call. Use a right and left grand, not a
weave the ring. Do NOT SWING the corner. Allemande left the corner and promenade
the original partner (do not swing). Use the figure in step 14 for all 4 figures
(no ladies progression, that is too confusing initially). You can alternate
heads and sides, and you can alternate the order: RLT then Prom 1/2, or Prom 1/2
then RLT. Any ring figure from step 10 can be used in ANY of the 7 parts of a
singing call. Review the advice on repairing a broken singing call in [Your
First Singing Call](03-your-first-singing-call.md). A broken singing call is
ESPECIALLY likely on these first tips with raw beginners.

Note that for BEGINNERS, REPETITION is a GOOD THING. Thus using the SAME figure
in a singing call is GOOD. It gives them more practice, and makes them feel
successful.

## Adding a Bit more Choreographic Complexity

After getting the first singing call down, the rest is just adding moves and
using them. These are the ones that people typically use, and they can go in any
order.

* **Ladies Chain** (heads, sides, all 4). You have already taught courtesy turn,
  you have tought the hard part already.   Start with just the heads. Each head
  girl shakes the other head girl's hand and pulls by, reaching out with the
  left hand (palm down). Meanwhile, the boys SLIDE into their partner’s position
  and reach out with the LEFT HAND (palm up). Tell the boys: if you SLIDE, the
  hand you reach out with is the obvious one, because the other hand is now far
  away and awkward to use. Finish the courtesy turn as before (the boys back
  up). The courtesy turn feels like a ¾ turn.
* **Ladies Center, men sashay**. Doing this more than twice feels repetitive,
  even for beginners, so the normal use is a four ladies chain, circle left,
  then Ladies Center, Men Sashay twice, allemande left, right and left grand. If
  you FORGET the four ladies chain, you can FIX it. Tell them to allemande left
  with the corner they have NOW (it is NOT the corner they started with), then
  right and left grand, but on the 3rd hand (the third person they take hands
  with) they promenade their original partner home.

The sequence

* 4 ladies chain across;
* 4 ladies chain back home;
* Face corner, AL, RLG, PROM

That sequence is another short figure you can use.

The sequence

```
4 LC, CIRC-L, L-IN-M-SASH, L-IN-M-SASH, AL, RLG, PROM
```

That sequence is a standard ring figure. You can use it anywhere you like in a
singing call.

At this point it is useful to teach them:

* **Weave the Ring**. I tell them the definition is a no-hands move, but
  beginners sometimes have trouble passing with no hands. So I always tell them
  to tap hands on the way by, which makes it feel like a right and left grand. I
  mostly teach weave the ring because I accidentally call it during singing
  calls.
* **Swing**. I only teach this if things are going well. It is handy because I
  will accidentally call this when I am performing a singing call (too much
  muscle memory). If I don’t teach it I will warn them to IGNORE it if I happen
  to say it. If I do teach it, I tell them that it is OPTIONAL, and that if they
  are CATCHING UP they should SKIP IT. To teach it, have the boys face promenade
  direction and the girls face the boys (this is where the dancers are right
  after a weave the ring). BOTH people hold out the arm that points to the
  center of the square (the boy’s left arm and the girl’s right arm). The boy’s
  other hand goes under the girl’s free arm and supports her back at the
  shoulder blade. The girl’s other hand rests on the boy’s shoulder. The boy and
  the girl should be OFFSET, with their right hips close. A swing is a full
  turn, walking around each other in a very small circle. If they are OFFSET,
  this should be EASY. After they can walk around once, they can try it again
  leaning out SLIGHTLY (the boy has to support the girl’s back to avoid losing
  balance). To finish the swing, the girl does one of these:
  * ‘Girls flop out.’ Let go of the hands that were pointing toward the center
    and open up until the girl is facing the same direction as the boy. Then
    start the promenade (they have to change hands).
  * ‘Twirl out.’ While the **boy walks forward, raise** the inside hand to form
    an arch. The girl walks through in a tight circle (it feels like a spin)
    until she is facing the same direction as the boy. **Let go.** Again, change
    hands to promenade position.

  It is up to the girl which one happens. The flop out is easier (good for
  beginners) and has less spinning. A swing plus a twirl is 2 revolutions for
  the girl. The twirl out lets the girl keep moving forward. The flop out
  requires a change of direction. Twirls also look better. Again, the girl
  decides.

  There is almost always a promenade after a swing. After they can swing in
  place, teach them to swing WHILE ALSO MOVING FORWARD. That gets them home
  faster, and they stay out of the way of the couple behind them. Instead of a
  tight circle, they take bigger steps and travel around the square as they turn
  around each other. That is the version you want them to reach.

* **Grand Square**. I used to think this was too hard for a
  no-experience-necessary dance, but experience has proven otherwise, and
  beginners REALLY get excited about it. I have seen it taught with just half
  the square and then combined. I don’t think this is necessary (and slows
  things down a lot). The key to helping dancers is to tell them
  * During this move you will ONLY look at your partner, OR your Opposite (NO
    ONE ELSE).
  * Every time you turn, you switch from your partner to your opposite, or from
    your opposite to your partner.
  * In particular you NEVER look OUT of the square (since you need to look at
    your partner or your opposite).
  * In each part, you take three steps and then TURN (to either your opposite or
    partner).
  * If you start out close to someone (you would run them over), you BACK UP,
    otherwise your steps are FORWARD.

  It is very useful if the caller calls 1, 2, 3, TURN for the first three parts,
  then 1, 2, 3, DON’T TURN, at the midpoint. Practice it WITHOUT music so you
  can go SLOWLY, and do it once SLOWLY before doing it to music. The first time
  you do it to music SLOW the music down (e.g. 124 BPM), and pick music with a
  good beat. Encourage everyone to clap on the turns, which keeps people doing
  it together.

This is more than enough to fill 2 hours. You will probably only need 4 singing
calls. The rest of the time is explanation and practice.

## Other Teaching Advice

Note that from an instructor’s point of view there is no difference between a
‘first lesson’ and a ‘no experience dance’. The first lesson is the hardest
(they know the least). The only other big thing with lessons is knowing [how to
resolve when the students know very few
calls](06-learning-to-resolve-a-square.md#resolving-when-the-dancers-have-had-only-1-or-2-lessons).
That is covered in the section on resolution. There is also the question of
teaching order for the calls. I ultimately [chose my
own](https://docs.google.com/document/u/0/d/1yKTBifePqiXljRpsuume4LY3OmmRFJSibnDL9_HQaTo/edit),
based on how common/important they were, but [Callerlab Mainstream
list](https://www.squaredance.org/topics/mainstream/Mainstream-2026.pdf) is
fine. I have used the [Mainstream Teaching
Guide](https://dancergram.com/a-00-Callerlab/Mainstream.pdf), and I like it (it
gives a lot of material that lets you call dances when they know very little).
Where I deviate is that the Mainstream guide teaches wheel around EARLY and uses
pass thru followed by wheel around instead of right and left thru. I have always
found that pointless. It teaches a low-priority call (wheel around) in order to
avoid an important one (right and left thru). I think they do this because they
think wheel around is easier, but they already teach ladies chain early, and
ladies chain is even harder than right and left thru, so dancers can handle
right and left thru. I teach right and left thru in two steps. First I teach
just the motions, a pull by and a wheel around. Then I add the hands. I still
CALL it right and left thru from the start. My main piece of advice is don’t be
afraid to change the teaching order. Teach the things that will ‘unblock’ the
choreography you WANT to call first.
